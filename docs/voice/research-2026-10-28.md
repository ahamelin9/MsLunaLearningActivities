# Luna's letter sounds: research notes

*Research run, 2026-10-28, for VOX-1 to VOX-4 (`kanban/epics/VOX.md`).
Research and notes only: nothing in the app, its voice files, the voice
scripts or their settings was changed.*

What a phonics sound should sound like is in the
[sound standard](sound-standard.md). These notes say why Luna's sounds fall
short of it today, what was measured, how a second open model did in a
bake-off, whether Alex should record the sounds himself, and what to decide
before round 3.

## In one minute

- **For letter sounds, the voice model isn't the problem; how the sounds
  are made is.** Luna's own speech is fine. Her R inside sentences is
  properly American (third formant down to about 1,930 Hz), and her L,
  vowels and hisses measure like a real woman's (but see her short vowels
  in single words, below). The isolated letter sounds go wrong in the
  cutting, holding and packing, in the eight ways in the table below.
- **Round 2 had a bug that hid some answers.** 12 takes came out 60–165 ms
  long, cut from the wrong stretch of voice. Every blend joined to an L
  used one of them. So round 2's "none right" for L, AR, OR, OO, long I and
  the L blends doesn't count against those methods.
- **Teachers have a precise recipe for every sound, and it maps onto
  audio.** B, D, P, T: replace the "uh" with a whisper. K, G: a short
  "radio static". R: the "fire engine". A blend: both sounds, run together.
  Demo clips of the whispered stops are on the Sound Check page.
- **The "TV static" is brightness, and it's measurable.** Every hiss Alex
  passed is within about 35% of real speech's brightness; every one he
  called static is over 40% (F, TH and V are 115–190% over). Compression
  doesn't change brightness, so it's the recipe, not the 40 kbps (which
  still costs some texture: 4.3 dB of spectral gap against 1.5 at 96).
- **A new problem, in words: Luna's short vowels drift when a word is said
  alone.** Short i lands where a real short e is ("chick" heard as
  "check"), short e leans to short a ("bed" as "bad"), and the "oo" of
  "book" isn't heard at all. Speed, spelling and punctuation don't move
  it, and every Kokoro voice does it (Luna's is among the clearest). It
  matters for phonics, where bed/bad and pin/pen are the pairs being
  taught. **A blend, Luna 70% with 30% of another Kokoro voice (Kore),
  raised Whisper's score on the app's 450 words from 355 to 388**, if it
  still sounds like Luna to Alex. Second pass, below.
- **The measures can screen takes before Alex listens**, as a gate (throw
  out the wrong sound) rather than a judge: on round 1 the gate threw out
  25 wrong takes and only one of Alex's picks.
- **A second model doesn't help.** Chatterbox, cloned to Luna's voice,
  got 1 of 17 problem words understood (Kokoro trimmed: 7). On short text
  it often says something else entirely ("Thank you."). Stay on Kokoro.
- **Voice conversion works on speech.** A man's reading came out near
  Luna's pitch and timbre with every word intact. That makes "record the
  hardest sounds yourself, converted into Luna" a real option; a ten-sound
  pilot would show how isolated sounds convert.
- **A finding about teaching, not sound:** children taught to blend without
  pauses ("mmmaaap") decode new words better than those taught with gaps.
  The app plays sounds with gaps. That's for a later ticket.

## Measured targets

**How.** I measured three sets the same way:

- 189 read sentences by 7 women from LibriSpeech dev-clean (mostly American
  volunteer readers, CC BY 4.0);
- 110 of Luna's own sentences;
- Luna's isolated letter-sound clips.

The sentences were lined up with their phonemes by the wav2vec2 phoneme
recognizer, and each sound was measured in Praat: the formants (F1, F2, F3,
in Hz) at its middle, and where its energy centres for a hiss. Formants are
what make a vowel, an R or an L sound like itself: F1 rises as the mouth
opens, F2 rises as the tongue moves forward, and a low F3 is what makes an
American R.

**Luna's voice itself measures like a real American woman.** Her R in
sentences (F3 down to 1928 Hz) matches the readers' (1958). Her vowels,
L, W and Y land in the same places. What's off is the isolated clips.

Sounds inside speech always measure lower than held ones, so this shows a
direction, not a target. The gap is biggest for F, TH and V, two to four
kHz brighter. Real F and TH are soft, flat breaths; Luna's are bright
hisses, close to how Alex described them ("TV static"). Round 3 has
"softer" takes with the treble eased off.

**Stops.** The voiced "uh" after the burst: B 60 ms, D 85, G 85, K 45; P and T
have none. Kilpatrick's target for B and D is "extremely short", and none at
all for P, T and K, replaced by a whisper.

**Lengths.** In reading, sounds are short (R about 40 ms, vowels 80–140).
Phonics holds them on purpose. Alex's picks put held sounds at 330–450 ms
(H 330, SH 420, S 450) and long vowels said slowly at about 370 ms.

## Bake-off: Chatterbox cloned to Luna's voice

**Verdict: stay on Kokoro.** Chatterbox (Resemble AI, MIT) was cloned to
Luna from a 25-second sample of her sentences and rendered 49 prompts twice
each (98 clips): held sounds written the way a teacher would ("Mmmmmm."),
the five letter names that were wrong both ways, 17 problem words and four
lines. Its clips carry Resemble's inaudible watermark.

| What | Chatterbox | Kokoro |
|---|---|---|
| Problem words (Whisper understood) | **1 of 17** (two takes) | 2 of 17 today, **7 of 17 trimmed** |
| Letter names (B, E, N, P, CH) | 0 of 5 | (wrong both ways in round 1) |
| Sentences (word-perfect) | 3 of 4 | already fine |
| Held sounds | Inconsistent: two takes of one sound often differ; several babble ("l-a-l-ow") | |
| Speed on this Mac | about 8–12 s a clip | about 1 s |

On short text it often says something else entirely: "Thank you.", "Okay,
hmm.", "Good bye then." That's a known weakness of this kind of model with
very short prompts. It also can't be given exact phonemes. Its sentences are
good, but Luna's already are. The takes are on the Research tab for Alex's
ear; numbers aside, a voice that occasionally says a different word is
wrong for a reading app.

## Should Alex record the sounds himself?

**Probably yes, for the sounds round 3 doesn't settle, converted into
Luna's voice.** Not before round 3: 22 of the 59 sounds already have a pick in
Kokoro (3 of them with a note), and round 3 has new methods for most of
the other 37.

**Why a person helps.** The sounds still open are mostly ones where a
voice model has nothing clean to cut from:

- B, D, G, T, K need a whisper, not a vowel;
- L and R don't exist on their own in speech;
- Kokoro devoices the voiced TH;
- V, Z, J, and NG with its G;
- the blends.

A person following the standard makes each one directly. The phonics apps
that sound right ship recordings, and developers report that TTS phoneme
markup (Azure, Polly) doesn't hold isolated sounds precisely.

**Why not use the recordings as they are.**

- **Two voices:** children would hear a man's voice for the sounds and
  Luna's for everything else.
- **Privacy:** the repo is public and the clips ship in the app, so his
  voice would be published. Today's `scripts/voice/recorded/` takes a
  recording as it is; raw recordings would need to stay out of git.

**Voice conversion fixes both.** It turns his recording into Luna's voice,
keeping his mouth's timing and shape. Chatterbox (installed for the
bake-off) has a voice-conversion model; tonight's test is below.
Unvoiced sounds (S, F, TH, P, T, K) carry almost no voice identity and pass
through nearly unchanged. Voiced, held sounds take on Luna's voice.

**The voice-conversion test worked on speech.** Four LibriSpeech readers
(two men, two women, about 4 seconds each) were turned into Luna with
Chatterbox's converter (`voice-audit/vctest.py`), about 5 seconds each:

| Reader | Pitch before → after | Overall F1 / F2 / F3 before → after | Words (Whisper) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Man 1 | 125 → 181 Hz | 385 / 1681 / 2623 → 491 / 1872 / 2941 | the same |
| Man 2 | 115 → 186 Hz | 535 / 1482 / 2700 → 553 / 1551 / 3040 | the same |
| Woman 1 | 240 → 215 Hz | 519 / 1753 / 2546 → 451 / 1809 / 2845 | the same |
| Woman 2 | 199 → 197 Hz | 564 / 1539 / 2820 → 503 / 1463 / 2930 | the same |

Luna's own sample: 198 Hz, 517 / 1878 / 2948. A man's voice moves most of
the way to hers and keeps every word. What this doesn't show is how
**isolated phonics sounds** convert: a held "mmm" or a whispered "t" is
short and unusual input. The way to know is a small pilot: Alex records the
ten hardest sounds (B, D, G, K, L, R, V, Z, J, NG), they're converted and
put on the Sound Check page beside Kokoro's best, and he decides from
there. The four conversions are on the Research tab to hear.

**How to record, if he does.**

- **Where:** the quietest room (a closet full of clothes is ideal), with no
  fan, AC or fridge hum.
- **With what:** iPhone Voice Memos set to Lossless (Settings → Apps →
  Voice Memos → Audio Quality), or a USB mic. Hold it 15–20 cm from the
  mouth, a little to the side, so P and B don't pop.
- **How:** each sound three times with a second's quiet between, then its
  word ("mmm … mmm … mmm … moon"). Hold the stretchy sounds about a second;
  whisper the quick ones, per the standard.
- **What:** about 45 sounds (short and long vowels, R vowels, stops, hisses,
  hums, W, Y, J, CH, X, QU, NG), plus the 20 blends if he likes; blends can
  also be built from the singles. About 20–30 minutes.
- **Then:** Claude cuts, levels and converts them, and puts them on the Sound
  Check page next to Kokoro's, the same way as the rounds. Nothing ships
  until he picks.

**The teacher** stays the backup (VOX-1): her sounds are the ones her
class hears, so they'd be the truest target, converted the same way.

Two findings along the way:

- Kokoro **devoices a final V or Z** said alone ("five", "buzz"): there's
  no pitch in it at all. Real speech does this partly too; the vowel's
  length carries the difference. It means no held V or Z can come from the
  end of a word.
- Cutting SP out of "spin" gives a P with no puff, which is correct inside
  a word but on its own is heard as "s-b". Joining Alex's S and P works
  better.

### Do the measures agree with Alex's ears?

Tested on round 1, where every card had 2–5 takes and Alex picked one:

- **As a judge, no.** Ranking a card's takes by the measures put Alex's pick
  first 10 times in 20 (chance: 6). For consonants the recognizer did well
  (5 of 7); for short vowels it failed, because all the takes measure
  alike: what Alex hears between them is naturalness, not which vowel it
  is.
- **As a gate, yes.** "A vowel's F2 within 20% of Luna's own, an R's F3
  under 2400 Hz" threw out 25 takes that were the wrong sound ("sit"
  drifting to "uh", "on" for O, "or" with no R) and only one of Alex's
  picks, the L he'd called "still a bit wrong", which measured as an "e".
- **Brightness predicts "TV static" exactly.** Every hiss he passed was
  within about 35% of real speech (S +19%, H +27%, SH +34%). Z at +42% was
  "still a bit TV static ish"; F, TH and V at +115% to +190% were "TV
  static". **Compression doesn't change brightness**: 40, 64 and 96 kbps
  measure the same. So the static comes from how the hiss is made; the
  bitrate is a smaller, separate question.
- **The recognizer can't gate blends or isolated stops**: it scores nearly
  all of them near zero, right or wrong. It's reliable for telling one
  consonant from another (D heard as J, SP heard as "s-b").

So VOX-4's check should fail a take on brightness (over +35%), on a
vowel's F2 or an R's F3, or on the recognizer hearing the wrong consonant,
and leave everything else to Alex.

**Predictions for the Research tab, written before Alex listens** (to
test the gate on round 3): F "from cuff, softer" (+24%; the others +52% to
+131%); TH "from tooth, softer" (+0.5%; the others +86% to +147%); V "from
van"; Z the mix with more hiss; voiced TH "from this"; W, Y, J and SP as in
the table above; NG "from king, with the i in front".

### Luna's short vowels in a word said alone

The first run checked each of Luna's vowels against real speech one at a
time, and they matched. It didn't check whether her short vowels stay
apart from each other. In the app's single-word clips they don't fully:

| Short vowel | Real women, words said alone (Hillenbrand 1995) | Luna, word alone (122 words) | Luna, the same word inside "Say ___ again." |
|---|---|---|---|
| i (sit) | 483 / 2365 | **746** / 2219 | 605 / 2178 |
| e (bed) | 731 / 2058 | 831 / 2012 | 905 / 1905 |
| a (cat) | 669 / 2349 | 1011 / 1937 | 1017 / 1841 |
| u (cup) | 753 / 1426 | 814 / 1722 | 785 / 1563 |
| o (hot) | 936 / 1551 | 901 / 1569 | 972 / 1314 |
| oo (book) | 519 / 1225 | **677 / 1540** | 484 / 1364 |

F1 / F2 in Hz at the vowel's middle. A higher F1 is a more open mouth.
Hillenbrand's speakers were from Michigan, where short a is raised, so
the short-a row is a loose guide; what matters is the spacing between
the vowels, and the two machine ears below.

- **Short i said alone sits where a real short e is** (F1 746 against
  731). The recognizer hears short-i words as "e" in 12 of 26; Whisper
  hears "chick" as "check", "chicks" as "checks", "windy" as "Wendy".
  Inside a phrase it's fine (1 in 26).
- **Short e leans toward short a**, alone (8 of 23 heard as "a": "bed" as
  "Bad", "net" as "NAT", "shell" as "shall") and even more inside a phrase
  ("pen" as "pan", "pet" as "pad").
- **The "oo" of "book" isn't heard as itself** in any of the 8 words
  ("cook" as "Kaki", "took" as "Toggy").
- Speed doesn't matter: the app's slow, normal and quick clips measure the
  same. Neither did `ᵻ` for `ɪ`, `e` for `ɛ`, or ending the word with "."
  or "!" (the "!" helped Whisper with short-e words, 20 of 23 against 16,
  but the vowel itself didn't move). Pitch alone and in the phrase is the
  same (217 and 219 Hz), so the formant measure isn't fooled by it.
- Cutting the word out of "Say ___ again." fixes short i and "oo" but
  isn't a fix as it stands: Whisper understood fewer of the cut words (74–77
  of 122 against 90), mostly from a bit of "again" left on the end
  ("Singing", "WEDDING").

- **Every voice drifts**; it's how Kokoro says a short vowel in a word on
  its own. Luna's is already among the clearest, tied with Bella and just
  behind Nicole (a breathy "headphones" voice). **Changing her voice
  wouldn't fix it.**
- The formants and the machine ears disagree on some voices: Kore's short i
  measures exactly right, yet it's heard shifted more often and Whisper
  understands fewer of its words. So F1 alone isn't the whole story.
  Duration, the glide inside the vowel and the consonants around it count
  too. A fix has to be judged by ear and by Whisper, not by formants
  alone.

**A blend of voices.** A Kokoro voice is a table of style numbers, and
two can be averaged (`blend-render.mjs`; at 100% Luna it matches her
clips exactly). Luna with 30% or 50% of Kore moves her short i to 670 or
600 Hz and her short e to 787 or 732 (real: 731). Bella barely moves it.
By Whisper, on the same short-vowel words plus the 8 "oo"-of-book words,
every blend helps:

| Version | Whisper understood (of 90) |
|---|---|
| Luna today | 71 |
| Luna 85%, Bella 15% | 76 |
| Luna 70%, Bella 30% | 78 |
| Luna 50%, Bella 50% | 76 |
| **Luna 70%, Kore 30%** | **80** |
| Luna 50%, Kore 50% | 78 |

Luna 70% / Kore 30% fixes 11 of those words and breaks 2. The fixes are
mostly the vowel mishearings: "ship" (was "Shep"), "chick" (was "check"),
"in" (was "End"), "ant" (was "and"), "cook" (was "Kaki"), "took" (was
"Toggy"), and also "hen", "jet", "six", "gets", "spent". It breaks "web"
("Webby") and "kitten" ("Kitchener").

**On all 450 of the app's words** (both rendered the same way, one take
each): Whisper understood **355 in Luna's voice and 388 in the 70/30
blend**. That's 48 words fixed and 15 broken. Besides the vowels, the blend
also clears many of the stray edge sounds VOX-2 is about ("A sheep",
"It stops", "a seed", "BUNZ" all come out clean). It breaks "apple",
"bird", "coat", "feet", "helped", "hot" ("Heart"), "kitten", "luna"
("Lunath"), "muddy", "sugar", "very" ("varied"), "warm", "water", "web"
and "yarn". One take each, and Whisper varies, so treat the size of the
gain as rough; its direction held on every set tried.
The Research tab has a listening test: two of Luna's lines
and six words (bed, net, chick, pin, six, book) in Luna today and three
blends, to hear whether a blend still sounds like her.

This matters more for these children than for most listeners: short e
against short a, and short i against short e, are among the contrasts
Spanish-speaking and many other ESL learners find hardest, and "bed"/"bad",
"pin"/"pen" are exactly the pairs phonics lessons drill.

**Also likely:** word-initial F is partly voiced in some words, which is
why Whisper hears "fox" as "Vox" and "fast" as "Vast" (69% and 76% of the
F voiced, against 24–43% for an S in the same place).

- **What Luna says wins; what's built from a piece of a word loses.**
  Every pick is Luna saying the sound slowly (the long and R vowels), a
  whole cut (J, Y) or a join of sounds he'd already picked (SP, SM). Every
  take made by holding a measured stretch of a word (L, R, V, Z, voiced TH,
  NG, the softer F and TH) "doesn't even sound like a letter", though most
  measured close to real speech. Holding a short stretch with PSOLA or
  grains changes what a listener hears far more than the formants or the
  brightness show.
- **The whisper works for P, T and K, not for B, D and G.** Whispering
  takes the voice away, and the voice is what tells B from P, D from T and
  G from K: a whispered B is, in effect, a P. The voiced stops need a trace

  and quiet as it can be, not a whisper.
- **Loudness.** Three of the stops he liked were "loud". VOX-1 already
  plans to level every sound by perceived loudness (LUFS) instead of raw
  level; these show why.
- **The predictions: 3 of 10 right** (Y, J and SP, SP with the shorter
  gap). The hits are consonants the recognizer can name. The seven misses
  are held hisses and hums chosen by brightness or formants. So the
  measures stay a gate that throws out a wrong take, never a way to choose

## For later: how the app teaches blending

- **Connected phonation.** Kindergartners taught to blend without pausing
  between sounds ("mmmăăăp" for *map*) decoded and read new words more
  accurately than those taught with pauses, and kept the gain on words that
  start with a stop (Gonzalez-Frey & Ehri, 2020; Reading Rockets). The
  pausers forgot the first sounds by the time they reached the last.
  `soundOutWord` plays each sound with a 260 ms gap, then the word. A
  stretched, joined version ("mmmaaap", then "map") is a teaching change,
  so it's for Alex and the teacher to decide, and it would be its own
  ticket.
- **Slower speech helps young listeners.** In toddler studies a slower rate
  improved word recognition. Alex already found "bed" and "ball" "too
  fast". Words are rendered at 0.95; 0.85 may suit K–2 ESL learners. A
  small change, but it re-renders every word.
- **Start with stretchy sounds.** Words that begin with a held sound (m, s,
  f) are easier to blend first; stop-first words come later. That's a
  content-order point for CUR-4.
